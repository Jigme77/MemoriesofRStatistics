Key Ideas from Statistics in Plain English

🔹 What is a p‑value?

A p‑value is the probability of obtaining your observed result (or one more extreme) if the null hypothesis were true. A small p‑value (typically < 0.05) suggests your data are unlikely under the null hypothesis, so you may reject it.

📌 In plain English: "If there really were no difference between groups, how likely is it I'd see a difference this big just by chance?"

🔹 Effect size matters

Statistical significance (p < 0.05) does not mean practical importance. Always ask: "How large is the difference?" Report means, confidence intervals, or Cohen's d — not just the p‑value.

📌 Example: "Angus cattle gained 0.21 kg/day more than Herefords (95% CI: 0.08–0.34, p = 0.003). This is a moderate effect (d = 0.62)."

🔹 Parametric vs non‑parametric

Parametric tests (t‑test, ANOVA) assume your data follow a normal distribution. Non‑parametric tests (Mann‑Whitney, Kruskal‑Wallis) use ranks and make no distributional assumptions — use them when normality is violated or with ordinal data.

🔹 Type I & Type II errors

Type I error: Concluding there is an effect when there isn't one (false positive).
Type II error: Missing a real effect (false negative). Larger sample sizes reduce Type II error risk.

Weekly plan & common difficulties

Week 1: t-test – cattle weight gain. Focus: p‑value interpretation.

Week 2: ANOVA – housing stress in hens. Focus: post‑hoc tests & effect sizes.

Week 3: Kruskal‑Wallis – dog pain scores. Focus: when to use non‑parametric tests.

Week 4: Chi‑square – breed & hip dysplasia. Focus: categorical data.

Week 5: Regression – milk yield & BRD risk. Focus: interpreting coefficients.
